# Journal Cover Letter Skill — Product Specification v2.1

## 1. Version objective

Version 2.1 is a major upgrade of the article-type-routed cover-letter skill, with the principal redesign focused on the `REVIEW_SYNTHESIS` branch.

The central change is conceptual:

> **A review cover letter must sell an interpretation, not merely report a synthesis.**

Version 2.0 correctly separated Research and Review workflows and imposed strict factual and claim-strength controls. However, its Review branch still tended to assemble correct components—field problem, evidence base, synthesis method, implications—without consistently converting them into a strong editor-facing thesis.

Version 2.1 therefore adds five capabilities:

1. diagnose the field-level misreading, unresolved tension, or inadequate interpretive frame that makes the review necessary;
2. derive one explicit, memorable **editorial thesis** from the synthesis;
3. distinguish scientific facts from higher-level editorial interpretation;
4. permit **controlled editorial uplift** so that the letter is persuasive without becoming scientifically inaccurate;
5. position the manuscript within the target journal's ongoing scholarly conversation rather than relying on generic scope matching.

The design benchmark is the comparison between two cover letters for the same systematic review manuscript:

- the expert-authored benchmark was more selective, more interpretive, more future-facing, and mildly more promotional;
- the v2.0-generated letter was more factually complete, more explicit about evidence boundaries, more journal-specific, and more declaration-complete;
- v2.1 must combine the expert's editorial reasoning with v2.0's evidentiary discipline.

The resulting design target is:

> **scientific literalism at the fact level + assertive interpretation at the synthesis level + controlled promotion at the editorial-significance level**

---

## 2. Locked product decisions

- **Runtime target:** Portable Agent Skill compatible with Codex and ChatGPT surfaces that support the Agent Skills format.
- **Domain scope:** Medicine and life sciences first, while remaining usable for other academic disciplines.
- **Article-type routing:** `ORIGINAL_RESEARCH`, `REVIEW_SYNTHESIS`, and `OTHER_OR_UNRESOLVED` remain separate branches.
- **v2.1 development priority:** The Review branch receives the major redesign; the Research branch remains active and must not regress.
- **Human-control gate:** Mandatory user confirmation after manuscript fact extraction, article-type routing, and story/thesis ranking.
- **Previous-letter handling:** Always ask whether a prior cover letter exists and how it may be used: format only; format and tone; maximum wording preservation; fact checking only; or benchmark analysis.
- **Default deliverables:** Final text, submission-ready DOCX, factual and declaration audit, journal and article-type audit, story/thesis audit, controlled-uplift audit, and unresolved confirmation items.
- **Length policy:** Do not impose a universal word-count target. Journal instructions and explicit user requirements take precedence. Otherwise, use the shortest length that fully performs the required editorial functions.
- **Page policy:** One page is a preference when natural and conventional, not a hard requirement unless requested by the journal or user.
- **Safety principle:** Never trade factual accuracy for persuasion.
- **Persuasion principle:** Never use factual discipline as an excuse for flat, defensive, or editor-unfocused writing.
- **Review principle:** Evidence coverage establishes credibility; the new interpretation earns editorial priority.
- **Uplift principle:** Scientific facts remain literal. Synthesis meaning and editorial relevance may be expressed at the strongest level reasonably supported by the manuscript and current journal evidence.

---

## 3. Product objective

Convert manuscript files, author confirmations, prior-letter evidence, and current journal evidence into a concise editor-facing submission argument.

The system must not merely summarize the manuscript. It must:

1. identify the manuscript's article type and submission pathway;
2. extract a verified manuscript fact sheet;
3. select the correct article-type narrative;
4. identify the single strongest editor-facing scientific or synthesis story;
5. rank genuine contributions rather than procedural features;
6. research current official journal requirements, readership, editorial leadership, and relevant publication patterns;
7. distinguish factual claims, synthesis interpretations, and editorial-positioning claims;
8. draft with calibrated confidence and controlled editorial uplift;
9. run bounded revision loops until scientific, journal, declaration, and persuasion gates pass;
10. output a submission-ready letter and auditable reasoning record.

For `REVIEW_SYNTHESIS`, the system must additionally answer:

- What is the field currently misunderstanding, conflating, overlooking, or interpreting inadequately?
- What does this review do to the evidence that previous studies or reviews did not do?
- What single new interpretation should the editor remember?
- What research, practice, policy, or governance decision changes if that interpretation is accepted?
- How does the manuscript advance a conversation already relevant to the target journal's readership?

## 4. Article-type router

### 4.1 Required classification

Before drafting, classify the manuscript as one of the following:

- `ORIGINAL_RESEARCH`
- `REVIEW_SYNTHESIS`
- `OTHER_OR_UNRESOLVED`

The user must confirm the classification before drafting.

### 4.2 ORIGINAL_RESEARCH includes

- observational studies;
- randomized or non-randomized intervention studies;
- laboratory, translational, clinical, epidemiological, computational, genetic, and mixed-method research;
- brief reports containing original data.

### 4.3 REVIEW_SYNTHESIS includes

- systematic reviews;
- meta-analyses;
- scoping reviews;
- narrative or critical reviews;
- evidence syntheses;
- state-of-the-art reviews;
- umbrella reviews and reviews of reviews when supported by the target journal.

### 4.4 OTHER_OR_UNRESOLVED

Protocols, methods papers, guidelines, case reports, data/resource papers, editorials, consensus statements, and other formats use a conservative fallback workflow. The skill must not silently force them into a Research or Review template. It must identify the mismatch and request confirmation or apply an explicitly labeled fallback.

### 4.5 Mixed or ambiguous manuscripts

When a manuscript combines a review with original data, method development, bibliometric analysis, or consensus work:

1. identify the primary editorial value;
2. identify the journal's submitted article type;
3. determine which branch best reflects the editor's expected decision logic;
4. ask the user to confirm the route before drafting.

## 5. Research article playbook

### 5.1 Editorial purpose

For original research, the cover letter should present a **scientific decision story**, not a compressed abstract and not a methods inventory.

The default narrative is:

> **Important problem → precise knowledge gap → design advantage → central finding → changed interpretation or consequence → journal-reader relevance**

### 5.2 Required research story elements

A Research cover letter must contain:

1. **Problem:** Why the scientific or clinical problem matters.
2. **Gap:** What specific uncertainty remains unresolved.
3. **Design advantage:** Why this study can address the gap better than prior work.
4. **Central finding:** The one result or result cluster that carries the submission.
5. **Meaning:** What the result changes in understanding, inference, or next-step research.
6. **Journal fit:** Why this finding belongs before this journal's readership now.
7. **Declarations:** Originality, simultaneous-submission status, author approval, conflicts, funding, and any required disclosures.

### 5.3 Research contribution hierarchy

Rank contributions in the following order unless the manuscript supports another order:

1. central discovery or answer;
2. design or evidence advantage that makes the answer credible;
3. mechanistic, subtype, or heterogeneity insight;
4. causal, predictive, translational, or methodological extension;
5. boundary or next-step implication.

Methods are not independent contributions unless the method itself is novel. A list of techniques must be converted into an explanation of what each technique adds to inference.

### 5.4 Research drafting rules

- State the central scientific story early.
- Include no more result clusters than are needed to establish the central finding chain.
- Prefer one coherent finding chain over exhaustive result coverage.
- Use quantitative results only when they materially increase credibility or distinctiveness.
- Explain method complementarity rather than enumerating analyses.
- Do not describe subgroup differences unless interaction or heterogeneity evidence supports the comparison.
- Do not turn statistical mediation into a proven biological mechanism.
- Do not turn internal model performance into clinical readiness.
- Do not call public data alone “full reproducibility” when code, environment, or workflow is not openly available.
- Do not equate a proxy or index with direct measurement of the underlying biological construct.
- Do not foreground the main limitation unless omission would materially mislead the editor.
- Place a necessary boundary after the positive contribution and frame it as calibrated scope, not self-rejection.
- Apply the general controlled-uplift policy in Section 7 without weakening research-specific evidence rules.

### 5.5 Research paragraph architecture

Default modular structure:

1. submission sentence;
2. problem and gap;
3. design advantage and central finding chain;
4. meaning, novelty, and journal-reader relevance;
5. declarations and signature block.

Paragraph count and length are adaptive rather than fixed.

## 6. Review article playbook — v2.1 redesign

### 6.1 Editorial purpose

For a review, the cover letter must show why the field needs a new interpretation of existing evidence—not merely why another search, update, or summary was conducted.

The default narrative is:

> **Field misreading or unresolved tension → synthesis intervention → new interpretation → changed decision or research agenda → journal conversation → calibrated evidence boundary**

The evidence base supports this narrative but should not dominate it.

### 6.2 Core review principle

A high-quality Review cover letter should make the editor understand:

> **The field has been viewing the evidence through an incomplete or misleading frame; this review changes that frame and therefore changes what should be concluded or done next.**

The letter does not need to accuse the field of being wrong. It may instead identify an unresolved tension, conceptual conflation, hidden heterogeneity, outdated assumption, or missing inferential step.

### 6.3 Mandatory review working state

Before drafting, populate the following internal state:

```yaml
field_misreading:
field_tension:
editorial_thesis:
synthesis_intervention:
evidence_credibility:
key_cross_study_pattern:
competing_review_difference:
decision_consequence:
future_agenda:
journal_conversation:
evidence_boundary:
controlled_uplift_level:
```

Definitions:

- **field_misreading:** the mistaken, incomplete, or overly broad interpretation currently encouraged by the literature;
- **field_tension:** the contradiction or unresolved uncertainty when a clear misreading cannot be established;
- **editorial_thesis:** the single editor-facing conclusion the letter is designed to make memorable;
- **synthesis_intervention:** how the review reorganizes, reclassifies, tests, or reinterprets the evidence;
- **evidence_credibility:** the methodological basis that makes the thesis believable;
- **key_cross_study_pattern:** the most distinctive pattern visible only or most clearly through synthesis;
- **competing_review_difference:** how the review differs substantively from recent relevant reviews;
- **decision_consequence:** what changes for research, practice, education, policy, or governance;
- **future_agenda:** the next-stage standard, validation pathway, or research priority defined by the review;
- **journal_conversation:** how the manuscript advances a discussion already relevant to the target journal;
- **evidence_boundary:** what remains unresolved and what the review must not claim;
- **controlled_uplift_level:** conservative, standard, or assertive, selected according to evidentiary support and user preference.

### 6.4 Field-diagnosis patterns

The skill should test for, but not force, the following patterns:

1. **Conceptual conflation:** two distinct constructs, outcomes, or levels of inference are being treated as equivalent.
2. **Unsupported extrapolation:** success in one setting or task is being generalized beyond the evidence.
3. **Fragmented interpretation:** studies appear inconsistent because they have not been organized by the correct framework, population, mechanism, context, or outcome.
4. **Hidden heterogeneity:** an apparently uniform literature contains important subgroup, methodological, contextual, or implementation differences.
5. **Wrong unit of analysis:** the field is evaluating isolated metrics or studies when the decision requires a pathway, chain, system, or longitudinal view.
6. **Outdated evidence model:** prevailing guidance or reviews no longer reflect the current evidence base.
7. **Implementation ahead of evidence:** adoption or recommendation is advancing faster than validity, safety, effectiveness, or equity evidence.
8. **Method–question mismatch:** commonly used methods or outcomes do not answer the decision that stakeholders believe they answer.
9. **Evidence asymmetry:** positive findings, negative findings, quality, or validity evidence are distributed unevenly in a way that changes interpretation.
10. **Missing decision boundary:** literature describes benefits and limitations but does not define where use is defensible and where it is not.

If none of these patterns is adequately supported, the skill must use a conservative Review narrative based on an unresolved field question rather than inventing a provocative diagnosis.

### 6.5 Review contribution hierarchy

For editor-facing prioritization, rank Review contributions in this order unless the evidence supports another order:

1. **editorial thesis or new interpretation;**
2. **synthesis intervention that produced that interpretation;**
3. **decision consequence or changed research agenda;**
4. **distinctive cross-study pattern or conceptual framework;**
5. **evidence-base credibility;**
6. **explicit evidence boundary.**

Evidence-base credibility is essential but is normally a support line, not the headline contribution.

### 6.6 Editorial-thesis generation

Generate up to three candidate editorial theses.

Each candidate must:

- be one sentence;
- be understandable without reading the full abstract;
- express an interpretation, not a process description;
- be traceable to the manuscript evidence;
- differ materially from recent competing reviews;
- imply a meaningful consequence;
- remain memorable after a brief editorial read.

Score each candidate on:

1. reframing power;
2. evidentiary support;
3. distinctiveness from existing reviews;
4. consequence for research or practice;
5. journal-reader relevance;
6. coherence with the manuscript conclusion;
7. memorability.

Reject any thesis that depends on:

- an unverified “first” claim;
- unsupported causality;
- unsupported comprehensiveness;
- assumed field consensus;
- unproven clinical, educational, policy, or implementation readiness;
- a distinction not actually demonstrated by the synthesis.

The user must confirm the selected thesis when alternatives are materially different or strategically important.

### 6.7 Synthesis intervention

The letter must explain what the review *did intellectually* to the literature.

Acceptable synthesis interventions include:

- organizing evidence through a theory or validity framework;
- separating previously conflated constructs;
- integrating quantitative and qualitative evidence into a new interpretation;
- identifying a cross-study gradient, pathway, asymmetry, or failure pattern;
- reconciling apparently conflicting findings;
- deriving a taxonomy, conceptual model, or decision boundary;
- comparing evidence across populations, contexts, methods, or implementation stages;
- testing whether a prevailing assumption is supported;
- converting fragmented evidence into a future validation or research agenda.

Routine search, screening, PRISMA adherence, database count, and quality appraisal are not synthesis interventions by themselves.

### 6.8 Evidence credibility as support

Use only the credibility features needed to make the editorial thesis believable, such as:

- review type and scope;
- number and type of included studies;
- search recency when timeliness matters;
- methodological quality appraisal;
- preregistration;
- meta-analysis or structured synthesis method;
- breadth of settings, populations, or evidence sources;
- triangulation across methods or outcomes.

Do not list all credibility features. Select those that directly support the thesis.

### 6.9 Changed decision or research agenda

The Review letter must explain what changes if the editorial thesis is accepted.

Possible consequences include:

- a different interpretation of current evidence;
- a revised standard for future validation;
- a more defensible implementation boundary;
- a change in which outcomes should be measured;
- a shift in research priorities;
- a new classification or reporting standard;
- a different policy or governance question;
- a clearer distinction between promising use and unsupported use;
- a roadmap from descriptive evidence to decision-relevant evidence.

Avoid generic endings such as “more research is needed.” Specify what type of research, validation, comparison, or decision standard is needed and why.

### 6.10 Journal-conversation positioning

The Review branch must distinguish generic fit from conversation-level fit.

Generic fit:

> The topic falls within the journal's scope.

Preferred conversation positioning:

> The journal's readers are already confronting a specific debate, and this review changes how that debate should be framed or advanced.

The skill should research:

1. official aims and scope;
2. article-type expectations;
3. recent relevant articles, editorials, reviews, or special discussions in the journal;
4. the journal's broad readership and decision context;
5. any explicit emphasis on conceptual advance, international relevance, translation, practice, policy, or methodology.

The fit sentence should state how the manuscript:

- complements;
- extends;
- challenges;
- reframes;
- consolidates;
- operationalizes; or
- moves forward

an identifiable journal-relevant conversation.

Do not call an observed publication pattern an official preference. Do not use unsupported phrases such as “aligns perfectly.”

### 6.11 Review drafting rules

- Lead with the field misreading, unresolved tension, or interpretive problem—not with database names or PRISMA compliance.
- State the editorial thesis early enough to govern the rest of the letter.
- Use the evidence base to establish credibility, not as the main story.
- Include only the strongest one or two cross-study patterns needed to support the thesis.
- Explain what the review changes in interpretation or decision-making.
- Distinguish the manuscript from recent competing reviews through intellectual contribution, not only search date or study count.
- Use future directions to define the next-stage agenda, not to append a generic request for more research.
- Position the paper within a journal conversation rather than relying on scope praise.
- Place the main evidence boundary after the positive contribution.
- Do not reproduce the abstract, table of contents, or full methods chain.
- Do not force every Review letter into an aggressive myth-correction narrative. Use a tension, gap, or reframing narrative when stronger language is not supported.
- Preserve active voice and authorial judgment.
- Use controlled editorial uplift where supported.

### 6.12 Review paragraph architecture

Default modular architecture:

1. **Submission and field diagnosis:** title, article type, target journal, and the field misreading or unresolved tension.
2. **Synthesis intervention and thesis:** what the review did intellectually, the minimum credibility evidence, and the central new interpretation.
3. **Consequence and journal conversation:** what changes for research or practice, why the journal's readers need the synthesis now, and one calibrated boundary if necessary.
4. **Declarations and signature block.**

The modules may be merged or separated. Paragraph count is not a quality criterion.

## 7. Controlled editorial uplift

### 7.1 Core principle

The target is neither maximal caution nor maximal promotion. The target is:

> **the strongest editor-facing interpretation that remains reasonably traceable to the manuscript evidence.**

### 7.2 Three-zone model

#### Zone A — Scientific fact zone

No uplift is permitted.

Includes:

- study counts;
- dates;
- designs;
- effect estimates;
- diagnostic or predictive performance;
- quality ratings;
- causal claims;
- subgroup differences;
- registration identifiers;
- funding, ethics, conflicts, and submission declarations;
- claims of firstness or comprehensiveness.

Every Zone A statement must be directly verifiable from the manuscript package or author confirmation.

#### Zone B — Synthesis interpretation zone

Moderate uplift is permitted when multiple findings reasonably support the interpretation.

Examples:

- identifies a systematic evidence asymmetry;
- exposes an unresolved validity gap;
- reveals a cross-study pattern;
- challenges an assumption;
- reframes inconsistent findings;
- distinguishes apparent performance from decision-relevant evidence;
- defines a previously unclear evidence boundary.

Zone B claims must be supported by the synthesis as a whole, not by a single isolated result unless clearly stated.

#### Zone C — Editorial significance zone

The strongest reasonable promotional language is permitted when it accurately describes the contribution rather than inventing a scientific result.

Examples:

- reframes the debate;
- provides a new evaluative lens;
- turns fragmented evidence into an actionable framework;
- establishes a methodological agenda;
- clarifies the next phase of validation;
- positions the field for more defensible implementation;
- advances an ongoing journal conversation.

Zone C statements must remain plausible, specific, and connected to an identifiable contribution.

### 7.3 Uplift levels

```yaml
conservative:
  Use when the evidence is exploratory, the synthesis is mainly descriptive, or the user prefers restrained language.
standard:
  Use by default when the review supports a clear cross-study interpretation and decision consequence.
assertive:
  Use when the conceptual advance is strong, competing-review differentiation is clear, and all high-risk claims remain controlled.
```

The user may request a more or less promotional tone. Tone changes must not alter Zone A facts.

### 7.4 Language calibration

Generally acceptable when supported:

- identifies;
- reveals;
- exposes;
- reframes;
- challenges;
- clarifies;
- distinguishes;
- consolidates;
- advances;
- provides a framework;
- defines a roadmap;
- establishes an evaluative lens or methodological baseline.

Use cautiously and verify:

- first;
- novel;
- comprehensive;
- authoritative;
- definitive;
- transformative;
- paradigm-shifting;
- implementation-ready;
- practice-changing.

Avoid unsupported journal praise:

- aligns perfectly;
- ideal for your prestigious journal;
- uniquely suited to your journal;
- guaranteed to interest all readers.

### 7.5 Uplift traceability test

For every materially promotional sentence, the skill must be able to answer:

1. What manuscript finding or synthesis operation supports this sentence?
2. Is the sentence describing a result, an interpretation, or editorial significance?
3. Would a reasonable editor view the wording as advocacy or as factual inflation?
4. Can the sentence be weakened without losing the real contribution?
5. Can it be strengthened without exceeding the evidence?

The preferred wording is the strongest version that passes all five questions.

## 8. General persuasion and claim calibration

### 8.1 Claim-strength ladder

Use verbs according to evidence strength:

- **Demonstrates / establishes:** reserve for designs and synthesis evidence that justify a strong conclusion.
- **Shows / identifies / reveals:** use for clear findings or well-supported cross-study patterns.
- **Provides evidence for / supports:** use for convergent evidence with residual limitations.
- **Suggests / is consistent with:** use for exploratory, indirect, heterogeneous, or weakly identified findings.

Revise both overclaiming and unnecessary hedging.

### 8.2 Persuasion-preservation requirements

Every draft must contain:

- one explicit novelty or contribution sentence;
- one sentence explaining why the work matters now;
- one journal-readership relevance sentence grounded in official scope or an accurately described publication conversation;
- one central contribution or editorial thesis that remains memorable after a brief read;
- one clear statement of what changes in interpretation, research, or practice.

### 8.3 Boundary placement

A necessary evidence boundary should normally appear after the main contribution.

Preferred logic:

> The synthesis provides [specific supported contribution], while showing that [specific unresolved boundary] remains the next validation or research priority.

Avoid opening the letter with the manuscript's principal weakness unless transparency requires immediate disclosure.

### 8.4 Anti-flatness rule

A draft fails persuasion review when it is accurate but primarily consists of:

- procedural description;
- study counts without interpretation;
- cautious verbs in every sentence;
- generic statements that the topic is important;
- neutral summary of benefits and limitations;
- scope matching without a journal-reader consequence;
- declarations occupying more rhetorical weight than the scientific story.

## 9. Adaptive length and compression policy

### 9.1 No universal target

Do not impose a fixed word-count range across journals, article types, and manuscript complexity.

Use this precedence order:

1. explicit journal requirement;
2. explicit user requirement;
3. submission-system field limit;
4. house convention if clearly documented;
5. otherwise, the shortest letter that fully performs the required editorial and declaration functions.

### 9.2 Length is a consequence, not a target

A letter is too long when:

- the thesis is diluted;
- the same contribution is stated repeatedly;
- methods or findings become a catalogue;
- the letter reproduces the abstract;
- journal fit is explained more than once;
- low-value declarations or contact details dominate;
- removing a sentence does not weaken the editor's decision basis.

A letter is too short when:

- the field problem is generic;
- the central finding or editorial thesis is not clear;
- the intellectual contribution cannot be distinguished from routine methodology;
- the reader relevance is asserted but not explained;
- essential declarations are absent;
- the evidence boundary or submission issue is materially omitted.

### 9.3 Adaptive compression and expansion

Compression should be triggered by redundancy, dilution, or abstract-like detail—not by an arbitrary word count.

Expansion should be triggered only when an essential editorial function is missing.

After any compression or expansion pass, re-run:

- fact audit;
- claim-strength audit;
- thesis/story audit;
- journal-conversation audit;
- declaration audit.

### 9.4 One-page handling

Use one page when it can be achieved without:

- removing the central interpretation;
- weakening the journal-conversation positioning;
- omitting required declarations;
- producing visually cramped formatting.

Do not shrink fonts, margins, or spacing merely to force one-page compliance.

## 10. Bounded loop architecture

### 10.1 Feasibility

Loops must be bounded, state-based, and auditable. Do not use uncontrolled “repeat until perfect” instructions.

Each loop must have:

- an entry condition;
- a state record;
- a maximum iteration count;
- explicit pass/fail criteria;
- an escalation or stop rule.

### 10.2 State object

Maintain an internal working state containing at least:

```yaml
article_type:
submission_branch:
fact_status:
critical_unresolved_items:
previous_letter_status:
previous_letter_permission:
ranked_contributions:
candidate_story_angles:
selected_story_angle:
field_misreading:
field_tension:
editorial_thesis:
synthesis_intervention:
competing_review_difference:
decision_consequence:
future_agenda:
journal_conversation:
evidence_boundary:
controlled_uplift_level:
length_basis:
hard_gate_failures:
quality_gate_failures:
iteration_count:
stop_reason:
```

### 10.3 Loop A — Intake and fact loop

**Goal:** obtain a reliable fact sheet and correct article-type route.

Process:

1. identify file roles;
2. extract facts and declarations;
3. identify the target journal and article type;
4. classify article type and submission branch;
5. identify missing or conflicting critical facts;
6. identify whether a previous letter exists and how it may be used;
7. ask only questions that cannot be resolved from files or official sources;
8. repeat until confirmed or explicitly accepted as unresolved.

**Stop condition:** article type, title, authorship, target journal, submission branch, central results or synthesis contributions, prior-letter permission, and required declarations are confirmed.

### 10.4 Loop B — Story or thesis selection

**Goal:** choose the strongest supported editor-facing narrative.

For Research:

1. generate up to three candidate scientific stories;
2. score each for importance, novelty, evidentiary support, coherence, and journal fit;
3. reject stories that depend on unsupported causality, subgroup differences, mechanism, or readiness;
4. select one central story and one secondary support line.

For Review:

1. generate candidate field diagnoses or unresolved tensions;
2. generate up to three candidate editorial theses;
3. identify the synthesis intervention supporting each thesis;
4. compare each thesis with recent relevant reviews;
5. identify the decision consequence and future agenda;
6. score for reframing power, support, distinctiveness, consequence, journal relevance, and memorability;
7. reject unsupported provocative claims;
8. select one central thesis and one evidence anchor.

**Stop condition:** one story or thesis clearly outranks the alternatives, or the user selects among materially different candidates.

### 10.5 Loop C — Draft, audit, and targeted revision

Maximum: three complete revision cycles.

Each cycle:

1. draft or revise the letter;
2. run all hard audits;
3. run article-type-specific audits;
4. run persuasion and controlled-uplift audits;
5. create a targeted revision plan;
6. revise only failed dimensions;
7. re-audit.

Do not rewrite the entire letter blindly after every failure.

**Early stop:** all hard gates pass and no material quality failure remains.

**Failure stop:** after three cycles, output the best safe draft plus unresolved failures; do not label it submission-ready.

### 10.6 Loop D — Adaptive length loop

Maximum: two passes.

Possible actions:

- compress redundancy;
- remove abstract-like detail;
- merge low-value method detail into credibility support;
- strengthen a missing thesis or consequence sentence;
- restore a required declaration;
- improve visual readability.

**Stop condition:** the letter performs all required editorial functions without unnecessary content and complies with any explicit length requirement.

### 10.7 Human-control points

Mandatory user confirmation:

1. after fact sheet, article-type classification, and ranked story/thesis proposal;
2. when critical facts remain unresolved;
3. before wording preservation from a prior letter;
4. when a high-risk claim is strategically important but evidentiary classification is debatable;
5. when the Review branch has two materially different field diagnoses or editorial theses;
6. when an assertive uplift level would materially change the tone or claim framing.

## 11. Audit gates

### 11.1 Hard gates

1. **Fact audit:** exact title, authors, article type, design, counts, dates, identifiers, findings, review characteristics, and declarations.
2. **Journal audit:** current scope, article type, readership, cover-letter rules, editor or salutation, and special pathway.
3. **Article-type audit:** the letter follows the correct Research, Review, or fallback narrative.
4. **Claim-strength audit:** causality, subgroup comparison, mechanism, generalizability, prediction, implementation readiness, firstness, comprehensiveness, and superlatives.
5. **Declaration audit:** originality, prior publication or preprint, simultaneous submission, author approval, conflicts, funding, ethics, related manuscripts, permissions, and required disclosures.
6. **Source audit:** manuscript facts come from the manuscript package or author confirmation; journal statements come from current sources; inferences are not presented as official policy.

Any hard-gate failure blocks `SUBMISSION_READY` status.

### 11.2 General quality gates

7. **Story audit:** one central scientific story or editorial thesis is identifiable.
8. **Contribution audit:** contributions are ranked, nonredundant, and decision-relevant.
9. **Persuasion audit:** novelty, importance, confidence, specificity, and reader relevance are sufficiently strong.
10. **Editorial-read audit:** the editor can identify the problem, contribution, credibility, consequence, and fit during a brief read.
11. **Adaptive-length audit:** no required function is missing and no material sentence is redundant.
12. **Tone audit:** the letter is confident and professional without becoming inflated, deferential, or defensive.
13. **Visual audit:** DOCX formatting is readable, conventional, and free of clipping, overflow, or artificial compression.

### 11.3 Research-specific checks

- subgroup difference claims require interaction or heterogeneity evidence;
- “mechanism” requires evidence beyond statistical mediation or association;
- “causal” must identify which exposure and outcome were causally tested;
- proxy or index constructs must not be presented as direct biological measurements;
- clinical guidance, precision intervention, implementation readiness, or practice change requires appropriate validation;
- reproducibility must distinguish public data, public code, executable workflow, and independent replication.

### 11.4 Review-specific checks

- Is the field misreading or tension supported by the literature rather than invented for drama?
- Is the editorial thesis an interpretation rather than an abstract summary?
- Does the synthesis intervention explain what the review did intellectually?
- Are evidence volume, evidence quality, and inferential strength kept distinct?
- Does the competing-review comparison identify an intellectual difference rather than only a later search date or larger study count?
- Is any “first,” “comprehensive,” “authoritative,” or “definitive” claim verified?
- Does a roadmap describe a future agenda rather than imply proven implementation?
- Is journal fit expressed as a reader-relevant scholarly conversation rather than generic praise?
- Are observed journal patterns clearly distinguished from official requirements?
- Is the evidence boundary placed after the positive contribution unless early disclosure is necessary?
- Does controlled editorial uplift remain traceable to the manuscript?
- Does the letter avoid collapsing evidence quantity, methodological quality, and validity into one vague claim?
- Are figure, table, template, license, and permission statements factually correct before claiming originality?
- Does the letter avoid forcing a provocative field diagnosis when only a conventional update or map is supported?

### 11.5 Controlled-uplift audit

For each materially promotional sentence, record:

```yaml
sentence:
zone: A | B | C
supporting_evidence:
claim_type: fact | synthesis_interpretation | editorial_significance
risk: low | medium | high
verdict: retain | strengthen | weaken | delete
```

A Zone A sentence cannot be justified by editorial enthusiasm. A Zone C sentence cannot contradict Zone A facts or the evidence boundary.

## 12. Editorial operations

Apply the following operations within revision loops:

- **Diagnose:** identify the field-level misreading, tension, or decision problem.
- **Select:** choose the one story or thesis that should drive editorial screening.
- **Reframe:** convert disconnected findings into a higher-level interpretation.
- **Anchor:** support the thesis with the minimum decisive evidence.
- **Merge:** absorb secondary methods and findings into a stronger credibility or inference statement.
- **Promote:** elevate the central discovery, synthesis insight, consequence, and reader relevance.
- **Uplift:** strengthen editorial interpretation without altering scientific facts.
- **Calibrate:** strengthen unnecessary hedging and weaken unsupported claims.
- **Position:** connect the manuscript to an identifiable journal conversation.
- **Bound:** state the evidence limit after the positive contribution.
- **Demote:** move routine reporting, search mechanics, secondary findings, and procedural compliance out of the main narrative.
- **Delete:** remove generic praise, duplicated fit statements, obsolete submission language, defensive caveats, and low-value details.
- **Reorder:** arrange content according to editor decision logic rather than manuscript section order.
- **Compress:** remove redundancy and abstract-like detail.
- **Expand:** add only a missing editorial function, not decorative language.

## 13. Core user journey

1. User uploads manuscript-related files.
2. Skill identifies file roles and extracts a manuscript fact sheet.
3. Skill classifies article type and submission pathway.
4. Skill asks whether a prior cover letter exists and determines permitted use.
5. For Research, skill proposes ranked scientific stories.
6. For Review, skill proposes field diagnosis, editorial-thesis candidates, synthesis intervention, and decision consequence.
7. Skill stops for user confirmation.
8. User confirms facts, route, story or thesis, target journal, declarations, tone, and prior-letter permissions.
9. Skill researches current official journal requirements, readership, editorial information, relevant journal conversations, and recent competing reviews.
10. Skill enters the correct drafting branch.
11. Skill runs bounded draft, audit, targeted-revision, and adaptive-length loops.
12. Skill outputs final text, DOCX, audit summary, story or thesis rationale, uplift audit, and unresolved confirmation items.

## 14. Source policy

### 14.1 Journal-source priority

1. official journal instructions for authors;
2. official aims and scope;
3. official article-type guidance;
4. official editorial-team page;
5. official publisher policy pages;
6. recent relevant content published by the journal;
7. official publisher or editor guidance on cover letters and editorial screening;
8. recognized secondary sources only for noncritical gaps;
9. third-party summaries only as labeled fallbacks.

### 14.2 Review-comparison sources

For competing-review differentiation, prioritize:

1. full text of recent directly relevant reviews;
2. journal-published abstracts when full text is inaccessible;
3. protocols or registrations only as supplementary context;
4. bibliographic records only when no better source is available.

Do not claim a competing review omitted a feature without checking the relevant source.

### 14.3 Internal labels

Internally label each journal-related finding as:

- explicit requirement;
- explicit scope statement;
- explicit article-type expectation;
- observed publication pattern;
- journal-conversation evidence;
- editorial inference;
- unverified claim.

Never call an inferred pattern an official preference.

### 14.4 Manuscript-source boundary

Manuscript facts must come from:

- uploaded manuscript files;
- uploaded supplementary files;
- uploaded prior letters or editorial correspondence;
- explicit author confirmation.

Journal research must not overwrite manuscript facts. A prior letter must not be treated as factually correct merely because it was human-written or previously submitted.

### 19.1 Benchmark comparison protocol

When a strong human-written prior letter is available, compare:

- what the human selected as the field problem;
- whether the human stated a stronger editorial thesis;
- how the human converted methods into credibility rather than content;
- how strongly the human promoted the interpretation and journal relevance;
- where the human overreached scientifically or omitted declarations;
- which rhetorical advantages can be retained without preserving factual errors.

The human letter is a benchmark, not a gold standard.

## 20. Acceptance criteria

Version 2.1 passes when:

- Research and Review manuscripts receive materially different, appropriate narratives;
- the Review branch produces an identifiable field diagnosis or unresolved tension;
- the Review branch states one clear editorial thesis that is not merely an abstract summary;
- the synthesis intervention is explicit and intellectually meaningful;
- evidence coverage is used as credibility support rather than the primary novelty claim;
- the letter explains what changes in interpretation, research, practice, policy, or governance;
- journal fit is expressed through a relevant scholarly conversation rather than generic praise;
- controlled editorial uplift improves persuasion without increasing factual or claim-strength violations;
- no hard factual, source, declaration, or claim-strength violation remains;
- strong words such as first, comprehensive, authoritative, definitive, and implementation-ready are verified or removed;
- the selected thesis remains identifiable during a brief editorial read;
- length adapts to journal and manuscript needs rather than a universal target;
- compression removes redundancy without flattening the story;
- the Research branch shows no material regression from v2.0;
- revision loops terminate deterministically;
- all major decisions and unresolved items are auditable;
- final DOCX output is visually submission-ready.

## 21. v2.0 → v2.1 change summary

| Area | v2.0 | v2.1 |
|---|---|---|
| Review core | Explain why synthesis is needed | Diagnose field misreading or tension and sell a new interpretation |

## 22. Governing principles

The final skill must operationalize the following rules:

> **A Research cover letter sells a finding and its consequence.**

> **A Review cover letter sells an interpretation and the decision that follows from it.**

> **Scientific facts remain literal. Editorial meaning may be assertive.**

> **Methods establish credibility; they do not replace novelty.**

> **Journal fit means advancing a reader-relevant conversation, not praising the journal.**

> **Length follows function.**

> **The strongest safe claim is preferable to either inflated promotion or unnecessary caution.**
