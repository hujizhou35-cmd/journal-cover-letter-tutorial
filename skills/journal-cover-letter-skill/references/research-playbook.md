# Original research playbook

## Editorial task

A research cover letter is not a shortened abstract. It is a rapid editorial decision aid. Its job is to let an editor understand, with minimal effort, why the problem matters, what current evidence or analysis fails to resolve, what the study changes, and why that change belongs in the target journal.

The letter should create one coherent scientific argument rather than display the full contents of the manuscript.

## Core decision spine

Build the scientific case around this sequence:

> Stakes -> unresolved limitation -> study response -> synthesized finding -> consequence -> journal fit

The sequence is conceptual, not a rigid sentence template.

### 1. Stakes

Foreground the specific scientific, environmental, clinical, technical, or policy problem that makes the study worth editorial attention. Avoid generic burden statements unless they directly establish the decision at issue.

### 2. Unresolved limitation

Define what prior evidence, prevailing assumptions, routine measurements, or conventional analytical approaches cannot adequately resolve. A strong gap explains a limitation in current inference or decision-making, not merely that “few studies exist.”

### 3. Study response

Explain what the study did differently at the level of capability. Translate methods into the question they enabled the authors to answer.

Prefer:

- “evaluated independent, cumulative, and interactive associations”;
- “distinguished temporal trajectories that cross-sectional analyses cannot resolve”;
- “tested the mechanism under environmentally relevant conditions.”

Avoid a catalogue of model names, instruments, software, or procedural steps unless one method is itself the principal innovation.

### 4. Synthesized finding

State one memorable conclusion or coherent finding cluster. Supporting results may be included only when they reinforce the same conclusion. Do not reproduce the Results section.

A finding cluster is coherent when its components answer one editorial question, for example:

- what conventional analysis missed;
- which component drove a joint effect;
- how an intervention changed the primary outcome and why;
- which mechanism explains an observed response;
- which subgroup or context changes interpretation.

### 5. Consequence

Explain what the finding changes in scientific understanding, measurement, risk assessment, practice, engineering, policy, or the next research decision. Choose the most immediate defensible consequence; do not stack every possible implication.

### 6. Journal fit

Connect the advance to a concrete scope element, readership need, or current journal conversation. The fit claim should answer why this journal's editor and readers can use the finding, not merely say the paper is “of interest” or “well aligned.”

## The contrast engine

Strong research letters often derive their persuasive force from a contrast:

> Current approaches imply or reveal X; this study shows Y; therefore Z should be reconsidered, measured differently, or investigated next.

Use contrast when the manuscript supports it. Do not manufacture a false opposition or claim that an approach “failed” when it simply answered a different question.

## Editorial compression rules

### Abstract methods into capabilities

Methods establish credibility and explanatory reach. Name individual methods only when doing so materially affects editorial judgment. Otherwise, describe what the combined design allowed the study to evaluate.

### Report at conclusion level

Default to a conclusion-level synthesis rather than effect-size inventory. Include a numerical estimate only when it carries the editorial argument, demonstrates unusual magnitude or precision, or is required to prevent an ambiguous claim.

### Select, do not catalogue

Use one central finding cluster and, at most, a small number of supporting facets. Omit findings that do not change the editor's understanding of the central contribution.

### Handle mixed or negative findings internally

Audit all null, mixed, subgroup, sensitivity, and exploratory results before drafting. Include them in the letter only when they define the central contrast, prevent a misleading synthesis, or materially qualify the implication. Never convert mixed evidence into an unqualified positive claim.

### Calibrate through verbs, not disclaimer paragraphs

Do not routinely copy the manuscript's limitations into the cover letter. Preserve boundaries through accurate verbs and nouns: “was associated with,” “identified a pattern,” “was consistent with,” or “suggests,” rather than causal or mechanistic language unsupported by the design. Add an explicit limitation only when the central pitch would otherwise be misleading.

### Keep administrative verification separate from scientific persuasion

Verify all declarations during intake. Include in the letter only declarations required by the journal, requested by the user, or standard for the submission branch. Do not insert funding, ethics, data, preprint, or AI statements merely because they were verified elsewhere.

## Paragraph functions

A typical research letter uses four editorial functions, though functions may be combined when brevity improves flow:

1. submission identification;
2. problem, unresolved limitation, and study response;
3. synthesized finding and consequence;
4. journal fit, required declarations, and professional close.

Each paragraph should perform a distinct function. If removing a sentence does not alter editorial judgment, remove it.

## Contribution hierarchy

Prioritize in this order when applicable:

1. the central discovery or inference;
2. the limitation in current understanding that the discovery resolves;
3. the design capability that makes the inference credible;
4. the concrete consequence for the field or journal readership;
5. secondary supporting results.

Methods, sample size, national representativeness, dataset prestige, and analytical sophistication are supporting assets, not substitutes for a scientific contribution.

## Common overclaims

- Association is not causation.
- Statistical mediation is not a proven biological mechanism.
- A proxy is not direct measurement of the construct it approximates.
- Significance in one subgroup and non-significance in another is not proof of interaction.
- Exploratory model importance is not an individualized intervention target.
- A variable selected across models is not automatically a biomarker, toxicant, mechanism, or regulatory target.
- Suggestive interaction patterns are not confirmed non-additivity without an adequate inferential test.

When such risks exist, retain the editorial value of the result while lowering the claim to the strongest supported rung.

## Research-route self-audit

Before delivery, answer:

- Can the scientific case be summarized in one sentence without listing methods?
- Does the gap identify a failed inference or decision, rather than a missing paper count?
- Is there one coherent contrast or discovery chain?
- Are the findings synthesized rather than enumerated?
- Does every technical detail change editorial judgment?
- Is the implication specific and no stronger than the design permits?
- Is journal fit explicit and concrete?
- Does the letter read as a pitch to an editor rather than an abstract, discussion section, or audit memo?
